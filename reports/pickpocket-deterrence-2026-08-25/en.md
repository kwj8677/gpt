# The City Where Getting Caught Feels Inevitable

## How Seoul made pickpocketing a bad business — and why Italy has not yet completed the same equation

> **Fashion changes the surface of a city. Policing changes its behavior.**  
> In 1990s Seoul, “watch your wallet” belonged to the vocabulary of crowded shopping districts. Today it barely does. Pickpockets did not become more ethical. **Their balance sheet changed.**

*Data-driven magazine column · 25 Aug 2026 · English edition*

---

### The thing that disappeared was not evil. It was profitability.

Contemporary reporting citing Korean police statistics shows recorded pickpocket incidents falling from **2,378 in 2011 to 535 in 2019 — roughly 77%**.

It is tempting to write a cinematic answer: *CCTV killed the pickpocket.* The research is more interesting.

A 2019 meta-analysis covering four decades of CCTV evaluations found a **modest but significant overall crime reduction of roughly 13%**, with better results when cameras were actively monitored and combined with other interventions. Cameras are not magic eyes. They are sensors inside a larger tracking system.

The real Korean transformation is better expressed as an offender's expected-value equation:

> **Expected payoff = loot × probability of success − expected cost of being caught**

Over three decades, every term moved against the thief.

### First: the wallet stopped carrying the product

Bank of Korea surveys show cash's share of payment transactions by count collapsing from **41.3% in 2013 to 26.4% in 2019, 21.6% in 2021 and 15.9% in 2024**.

Cash is unusually attractive to a street thief: liquid, anonymous and instantly spendable. Cards can be frozen. Phones generate identifiers, accounts and location traces. Electronic payments leave records.

A U.S. quasi-experiment reinforces the mechanism. When welfare payments shifted from paper instruments to Electronic Benefit Transfer cards, an NBER study estimated an **approximately 9.8% decline in overall crime**, with significant reductions in burglary and larceny as well. It does not prove the Korean case, but it shows that shrinking anonymous street cash can change crime economics.

Seoul did not merely make wallets safer. It made them **less worth stealing**.

### Second: “I probably won't be caught” became harder to believe

The U.S. National Institute of Justice's synthesis of deterrence research makes one point unusually clear: **certainty of apprehension is a much stronger deterrent than simply increasing the severity of punishment**.

Offenders do not generally study sentencing tables before reaching into a bag. But they can learn that a face caught at one station can be matched to footage at another, that repeated incidents can be linked, and that escape routes can become evidence.

A 2023 Korean case shows the loop in miniature: repeated market theft reports led police to compare appearance and movement patterns, conduct surveillance and arrest a repeat offender in the act.

The deterrent machine is not “camera → arrest.” It is:

**report → footage → route → identity → pattern → targeted surveillance → arrest**

That closed loop is the physical foundation beneath the social intuition: **“You do this here, you eventually get caught.”**

### Italy is not doing nothing

Italy's railway police deployed more than **42,000 patrols in 2025** and ran **8,526 dedicated anti-pickpocket services** in stations and trains. More than 4.8 million passengers were checked.

Yet Istat reports a 2024 pickpocket-victimisation rate of **5.1 per 1,000 inhabitants**. That is better than 2014's 6.9, but almost unchanged from the previous year. In tourism-heavy nodes, exposure is more concentrated than the national rate suggests.

So the puzzle is not “Where are the police?”

The puzzle is: **Why does the business model survive despite the policing?**

### Italy's friction lies between “caught” and “consequence”

The 2022 Cartabia reform expanded the number of offences requiring a victim's formal complaint — *querela* — before prosecution can proceed. Ordinary theft under article 624 entered that framework.

Official Italian judicial analysis openly describes the logic as **procedural deflation**: let the victim's choice filter which cases consume scarce court capacity.

That can be rational for an overloaded justice system. Tourist pickpocketing is a hostile edge case.

Victims may leave the country quickly. And under the post-reform procedural framework, when a complainant is formally summoned as a witness and fails to appear without justified reason, that absence can — where the law permits — count as tacit withdrawal of the complaint.

This does **not** mean every tourist must physically return to an Italian courtroom. It does mean that the victim's continued procedural participation can matter in a class of crime where the victim is unusually transient.

If deterrence depends on certainty, any friction that reduces the perceived probability of a final consequence matters.

### Italy has already begun correcting course

In 2023, lawmakers patched rules around flagrante arrests when the victim could not immediately be found.

Then, in April 2026, Italy amended Penal Code article 624-bis to treat certain deft thefts involving **payment instruments, identity documents, digital devices, mobile phones, or sufficiently valuable property** more seriously. Italy's Constitutional Court in 2026 also confirmed the ex-officio nature of article 624-bis proceedings.

Read the sequence as policy telemetry:

**2022:** reduce court load.  
**2023:** don't let procedural design cripple obvious arrests.  
**2026:** modern pickpocket targets need a harder category.

That is not proof of indifference. It is evidence of a system **re-optimising after discovering an incentive problem**.

### What Seoul appears to have learned earlier

To kill pickpocketing, do not begin with “How many years in prison?”

Begin with: **Can this still make money?**

Korea pushed several variables in the same direction:

**cash ↓  
anonymous loot ↓  
video traceability ↑  
repeat-offender linkage ↑  
digital payment records ↑  
perceived certainty of identification ↑**

Italy has strong police activity too, but tourist density, repeat opportunities, procedural friction and uneven local implementation have historically prevented those pieces from producing the same perceived inevitability.

More officers can arrest more offenders. A better system makes offenders **self-select out before the attempt**.

### What the data do not prove

This is not a causal paper claiming “CCTV caused a 77% fall in Korean pickpocketing.”

Korea's decline could also reflect cashlessness, card adoption, smartphone traceability, transport surveillance, investigative practice, demographic change, offender ageing and displacement into online fraud. Nor should Korean police incident counts be directly ranked against Italian victimisation rates.

But the mechanism-level evidence converges:

- deterrence research privileges **certainty over severity**;
- CCTV performs better as part of a **monitored, multi-intervention system**;
- less anonymous cash can reduce the returns to street theft;
- Korean pickpocket incidents and cash use both collapsed over the same broad era;
- Italy's own legal trajectory shows a move from complaint-based procedural deflation toward stronger treatment of modern deft theft.

Different data sets, same machine.

## The final scene

In 1990s Seoul, the pedestrian held the wallet tighter.

In modern Seoul, the offender has to ask first:

**“How much cash is even in there?”**  
**“The card gets frozen.”**  
**“The phone is traceable.”**  
**“How many cameras saw me before I even touched it?”**

The highest form of public safety is not a city where police heroically catch every thief.

It is a city where the thief runs the numbers — and decides the job is stupid.
